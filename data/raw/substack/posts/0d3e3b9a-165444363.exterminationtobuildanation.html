The Canadian Pacific Railway, completed in 1885, is often mythologized as the steel thread that stitched together a fragile Confederation. Politicians claimed it was the only way to bind British Columbia to the rest of Canada. Businessmen saw profit. Settlers saw opportunity. The state saw a tool for sovereignty. But beneath the patriotic narratives lies a darker truth; one of dislocation, environmental upheaval, and broken promises to Indigenous peoples.

Last Breath of the Buffalo

It was 1879, and the sun hung low over the shortgrass prairie near what is now Medicine Hat, Alberta. The wind curled through the coulees, carrying no sound; no hoofbeats, no snorts, no thunder of life. The great herds were gone.

Just a decade earlier, this land had shaken under the weight of tens of thousands of buffalo, moving like a dark wave across the earth. The Plains Cree, who had followed the herds for generations, knew these rhythms as intimately as breath and heartbeat. But now the land was silent, scraped clean by men who came with rifles, trains, and hunger for empire.

Downwind, just beyond the coulee ridge, a group of European-American hunters moved methodically. They were from Montana, but had pushed north into Canadian territory with the promise of easy kills. Their camp smelled of gun oil, grease, and sun-rotted hide. Buffalo robes were stacked like cordwood, thick with flies.

They were not hunting for food. They were hunting for extinction. Each man carried a .50-calibre Sharps rifle, the infamous “buffalo gun.” From 800 yards out, the weapon could split a beast’s spine. Spot a herd, shoot the leaders, and wait. A good team could kill a hundred buffalo in a day. They would take only the hides, perhaps the tongues, and leave the rest to rot.

The stench of it blew eastward, toward a Cree winter camp near the South Saskatchewan River. Women boiled bark and moss. Children cried through the night. Elders told stories of days when the earth moved with life. Hunters returned with nothing but dry feet and bitter news.

In government dispatches, the news was cold and calculated. Indian Commissioner Edgar Dewdney kept food stores locked in Fort Walsh, rationing them only to chiefs who agreed to Treaty 6. 'Give them nothing until they are on the verge of starving,' one bureaucrat advised. 'Then they will sign.'

In the U.S., General Sheridan was more blunt: 'Kill every buffalo you can. Every buffalo dead is an Indian gone.'

That night, a Cree hunter watched the red sky sink. He had not fired his musket in days. His horse was bone-thin. He saw vultures circling the settler kill site, picking flesh from torn carcasses. The meat, once sacred, now left to spoil. A boy asked him when the buffalo would return. He did not answer.

A Manufactured Collapse

The extermination of the North American plains bison (buffalo) in the 19th century was not an ecological accident, it was an intentional act, one that had devastating consequences for the Plains Cree, Blackfoot and Assiniboine. The extermination of buffalo severed the foundations of their economies, cultures, and sovereignties, and paved the way for the Canadian Pacific Railway expansion and settler colonization.

In the early 1800s, buffalo herds on the plains numbered 30–60 million. By the 1880s, they were virtually extinct in Canada, with fewer than 500 animals remaining in the wild.

Colonial officials encouraged buffalo extermination to starve Indigenous populations into submission. The Canadian government allowed the collapse of the herds to facilitate treaty signings and land surrender.

As the buffalo disappeared in the 1870s and 1880s, the Plains Cree entered a period of crisis. Without buffalo, they faced starvation, disease, and coercion from the government, which withheld food rations to force treaty signings.

"We were rich, my people, but you took away our richness. We had plenty of buffalo, plenty of everything. But now we are poor." — Chief Big Bear, 1885 trial statement

"We do not want to sign away our land. We want the government to listen to us as a people. We want to live as we did before, following the buffalo and praying to the spirits." — Attributed to a Cree speaker, Treaty 6 discussions, Fort Pitt, 1876.

Tracks of Displacement

The construction of the Canadian Pacific Railway was not simply a technical or economic project, it was a deliberate act of colonization. To make way for the tracks, the Canadian government needed to secure control over vast swaths of Indigenous land. This meant removing the original inhabitants through a combination of coercive treaties, forced relocation, and legal restrictions on Indigenous mobility and resistance.

Massive land acquisitions were secured through treaties that were often signed under pressure, misunderstood, or outright violated. In British Columbia, most of the land the railway traversed was never ceded at all. In the Prairies, the Crown rushed to sign treaties (like Treaties 1–7) before track-laying began, aiming not at fair agreements but at clearing Indigenous people from land for settlers and rail access.

The Indian Act (1876) and the aggressive enforcement of the pass system helped prevent Indigenous resistance, ensuring people could not move freely near the rail lines, even on their own traditional territories.

In the 1870s and 1880s, just as plans for the Canadian Pacific Railway were solidifying, the federal government rushed to negotiate Treaties 1 through 7, covering most of what is now Manitoba, Saskatchewan, and Alberta. These treaties were ostensibly peaceful agreements between the Crown and Indigenous Nations, including the Anishinaabe, Cree, Assiniboine, and Blackfoot Confederacy. In reality, many were signed under duress and deception:

Treaty 6 (1876) was signed by the Plains and Woods Cree in parts of central Alberta and Saskatchewan. Chief Mistahimaskwa (Big Bear) of the Plains Cree initially refused to sign, stating that the terms were insufficient and likening the forced settlement onto reserves to "pulling a rope around a person’s neck, denying them freedom on their land." However, facing starvation due to the collapse of the bison population, Big Bear was compelled to sign an adhesion to the treaty in 1882. This was not negotiation, it was coercion through hunger.

Treaty 7 (1877) was signed at Blackfoot Crossing by the Blackfoot Confederacy (Siksika, Kainai, Piikani), the Nakoda (Stoney), and the Tsuut’ina nations. Their leaders believed they were agreeing to share the land, not surrender it entirely. Chief Crowfoot of the Siksika Nation reportedly said, "The Queen's men said we would always sit on softest feathers," reflecting the belief that the treaty would ensure their well-being. Crucially, the treaty was signed after surveyors and railway scouts had already begun mapping routes, the deal was a formality to justify what had already begun.

Meanwhile, in British Columbia, where the Canadian Pacific Railway would eventually reach the Pacific, no treaties were signed at all across the vast majority of the territory. The tracks crossed through unceded territories of nations like the Secwépemc, Nlaka’pamux, Stó:lō, Tsilhqot’in, and many others, whose rights were simply ignored by colonial governments.

Once land was secured, or presumed to be, the state turned to control and containment of Indigenous peoples. The Indian Act of 1876 created a legal framework that redefined Indigenous nations as wards of the state. One of the most oppressive tools developed under this framework was the pass system.

Though never codified in law, the pass system was widely enforced from the 1880s through the 1940s, particularly on the Prairies. Under this system, Indigenous people living on reserves were required to obtain a written pass from an Indian agent to leave their community. No pass? You could be detained or forcibly returned to your reserve.

This was a direct response to growing Indigenous resistance, including the 1885 Northwest Resistance led by Louis Riel and supported by Cree and Métis allies. The Canadian state feared further uprisings, especially along the vulnerable railway corridor, so it used the pass system to isolate communities, suppress political organization, and disrupt cultural continuity.

Indian agents, backed by the North-West Mounted Police (now RCMP), monitored railway routes and enforced movement restrictions. In many areas, Indigenous people were forbidden from crossing rail lines, even when the tracks ran through their own territories. This ensured the CPR remained secure, and kept Indigenous presence out of settler sightlines.

The combined effect of these policies was profound: entire nations were dispossessed, starved into submission, and confined. Lands that had been stewarded for millennia were turned into survey grids, towns, and rail sidings, without consent and without compensation.

Today, the echoes of that displacement still reverberate. Many Indigenous communities along the railway continue to face poverty, environmental degradation, and limited land rights, even as Canadian Pacific Kansas City (formerly Canadian Pacific Railway) profits from routes laid atop their ancestors’ lands. First Nation communities along the line face dangerous rail crossings, no profit-sharing, and no say in how the lines are maintained or expanded, while the rail company has profited massively from land granted in the 19th century, land that in many cases was never the Crown’s to give.

A Country Worth Connecting

This story of buffalo bones and steel rails is not just history, it is the foundation beneath every pipeline, highway, transmission line, and railway that follows. The Canadian Pacific Railway didn’t just connect a country; it dispossessed nations, and built wealth on the ashes of economies it helped destroy.

As Canada faces a new era of infrastructure expansion, for energy corridors, critical minerals, green transportation, and climate adaptation, the question is no longer just what we build, but how and with whom.

True reconciliation demands more than land acknowledgments or one-off consultations. It requires structural shifts in how infrastructure is financed, governed, and owned; decision-making power that sits with Indigenous governments, not above them; equity partnerships where First Nations are not stakeholders, but rights-holders and co-owners; and projects must deliver long-term generational benefit, not just short-term mitigation.

There are examples emerging: Indigenous-led transmission projects in Ontario and Manitoba, majority-First-Nation ownership of pipelines in Alberta, and co-developed and co-owned clean energy ventures in the North. These are not just reconciliatory gestures, they are models of economic justice, environmental stewardship, and political renewal.

But the legacy of the Canadian Pacific Railway reminds us what happens when infrastructure is used to erase, rather than include. The story we tell about the past shapes what we imagine for the future.

The rail line united Canada, yes, but it also divided Nations, lands, lives, and laws in ways that echo into the present. If Canada is to build again, it must do so not on the backs of Indigenous Nations, but in full partnership with them. That is how we make the land whole again. That is how we make a country worth connecting.
