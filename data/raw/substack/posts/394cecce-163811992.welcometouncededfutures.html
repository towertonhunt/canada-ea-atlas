Welcome to Unceded Futures — Revised

A note on this post: This is an updated version of the launch post from June 2025. The publication has now been running for nine months. This revision reflects more clearly what Unceded Futures is, who it’s for, and where I stand in relation to it.

Who I Am and Why That Matters

I am a settler. I was born and raised in Canada, I built a career in this country’s resource and infrastructure sector, and I have benefited from systems that were built, in part, on the dispossession of Indigenous peoples. That is not a confession. It is a starting point.

For over twenty years I worked as an environmental consultant conducting impact assessments for resource projects across Canada. Mines, pipelines, transmission lines, dams. I reviewed plans, studied land, and engaged communities, both settler and Indigenous, in processes designed to evaluate whether a project should proceed and under what conditions.

The longer I worked in that field, the harder it became to ignore a pattern. The projects got approved. The assessments got completed. The consultation boxes got checked. And the communities that had been living on and with that land for generations often ended up with less than they started with, or with promises that dissolved once the shovels were in the ground.

I sold my consulting firm in 2024. Unceded Futures is what I started instead.

What This Publication Is

Unceded Futures examines infrastructure, land, and power. Each post focuses on a specific project, past or present, a railway, a pipeline, a dam, a military base, and asks the same set of questions: How did this get built? Who was on the land when it happened? Were they consulted, compensated, or simply removed? What did they do about it? And what are the legal and political consequences that are still playing out today?

The posts are grounded in the legal arc of Indigenous rights in Canada, from the Calder decision in 1973, which first recognized Aboriginal title in Canadian law, through the Berger Inquiry, Sparrow, Delgamuukw, Haida Nation, Tsilhqot’in, and Yahey. Each of these cases moved the line on what governments are legally required to do before acting on Indigenous land. The posts trace that arc case by case, project by project, and show where the law has moved and where the gap between law and practice remains wide.

This is not an Indigenous publication. I want to be clear about that. Unceded Futures does not speak for Indigenous communities or claim to represent their perspectives. It is a settler’s account of settler conduct, examined through the legal and historical record, with the aim of making that record harder to ignore.

Why a Settler Should Write This

It is a fair question. There are Indigenous writers, scholars, lawyers, and journalists doing this work with authority I don’t have. I read them and I recommend them and I will link to them wherever I can.

What I can add is a specific vantage point: someone who spent two decades inside the environmental assessment process, who has sat in community consultation meetings and read the regulatory filings and watched the gap between the Crown’s stated commitments and its actual conduct. That experience does not make me an authority on Indigenous rights or Indigenous experience. It does make me a witness to how the system works, and to how often it doesn’t.

The argument for writing this as a settler is simple. Reconciliation is not only Indigenous peoples’ work. The history of how this country was built is not only Indigenous peoples’ story to tell. Settlers have an obligation to understand it, to account for it, and to change the systems they operate in. This publication is my attempt to do that in the medium I know best.

Why Now

Two pieces of proposed legislation frame the present moment. Bill C-5 at the federal level and Bill 5 in Ontario are both aimed at accelerating infrastructure approvals by streamlining environmental assessments and reducing consultation requirements. The arguments for them are familiar: projects take too long, investment is being lost, Canada needs to move faster.

The posts in this series are a response to that argument. Not by opposing infrastructure, but by asking what happens when governments have moved fast before, without consent, without adequate process, and without honouring their commitments. The answer is in the record. It is not reassuring.

What’s Been Published

Since June 2025, Unceded Futures has published six posts:

The Canadian Pacific Railway and the dispossession of Indigenous peoples across the prairies and BC. The Canol Pipeline, built in 13 months on Sahtú Dene territory under the War Measures Act and abandoned, leaving a toxic legacy the community is still cleaning up. The Mackenzie Valley Pipeline inquiry, where Justice Thomas Berger spent three years listening to Dene and Métis and Gwich’in communities before recommending that no pipeline be built until land claims were settled. The James Bay hydroelectric project and the Cree and Inuit resistance that produced Canada’s first modern land claims agreement. The mercury poisoning at Grassy Narrows and the fifty-year fight for cleanup and accountability. And Ipperwash, where the Ontario government took land under wartime emergency powers, broke a fifty-year promise to return it, and then sent police when the families moved back.

Each post is linked in the archive. If you’re new here, the Mackenzie Valley post is a good starting point. It’s the story of how environmental assessment in Canada was born, and what it was designed to prevent.

What’s Coming

The next posts will cover the Wet’suwet’en and the Coastal GasLink Pipeline, the Tsilhqot’in Nation and the Prosperity Mine, the Elsipogtog fracking blockade in New Brunswick, Site C, Lubicon Lake, and the Ring of Fire in northern Ontario. Each one follows the same structure: a specific project, a specific community, a specific legal and political turning point, and a bridge to the present.

One post per month.

Who This Is For

Environmental assessment and resource industry practitioners who want to think more critically about the systems they work in. Students and educators in environmental studies, law, and Indigenous studies. Journalists and policy researchers. Community members and advocates seeking historical context and case law. And anyone who believes that future infrastructure in Canada should be built differently than it has been built before.

If that’s you, subscribe. It’s free. It will stay free.
