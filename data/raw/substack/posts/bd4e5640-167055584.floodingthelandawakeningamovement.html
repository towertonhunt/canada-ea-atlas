The Day the Strangers Came: A Cree Story of the First Hydro-Québec Arrival

I remember it was late summer, the kind of day when the moss feels warm under your boots and the sound of geese fills the sky. We were in Waskaganish, the old people sitting by the fire, the young ones out setting snares or checking the water for fish. It had been quiet that season, no trouble, no big stories. Until the strangers came.

They arrived by floatplane, their engines breaking the stillness of the bay. Some of the men had been out on the land when they spotted the planes circling low, making a racket over the rivers. Not many outsiders came up this way; we knew the Hudson’s Bay post, the missionaries, sure, but this was different. These ones didn’t come to trade or preach. They came with maps, blueprints, and the look of people who had already decided what was going to happen.

They called themselves Hydro-Québec, some from the government too. They came ashore in shiny boots with rolled-up maps under their arms, telling us they were here to bring progress. They talked fast, in French and English, but not Cree. Their words didn’t sit right with us; they spoke of dams, roads, and power lines, like they already owned the rivers and the forests.

I remember the look on the old hunters’ faces when they spread the maps on the table in the band office. Lines drawn over the land as if our rivers were theirs to move, our lakes theirs to flood. The La Grande, they said, was going to be turned into a mighty source of electricity, for the good of Quebec, for the good of everyone. But they weren’t talking about us.

No one had come to ask permission. They hadn’t sat with the elders, shared tea, listened to the stories of the land. They hadn’t seen the traplines, the burial sites along the shores, the places where we taught our children to hunt and fish. They saw empty space on a map, but we saw home.

At first, some thought maybe it was just more talk, but then the bulldozers came. Planes dropped supplies. Roads were cut through the forest without so much as a knock on the door. That’s when we understood this wasn’t just talk; they were already carving up Eeyou Istchee.

The elders called meetings, the young people listened closely. We decided we couldn’t let them take the land without a fight. It wasn’t just about hunting or fishing, it was about survival. If the rivers changed, everything changed. The fish, the beavers, the birds, even the seasons themselves would be different.

That was the beginning, the moment the outside world forced its way in. But it was also when we found our voice, when the people began to stand together. We didn’t have much in the way of lawyers or politicians back then, but we had the stories of our ancestors, the knowledge of the land, and the will to protect it.

We didn’t ask for this fight, but we took it up all the same.

The James Bay Hydroelectric Project: Quebec’s Northern Ambitions

Long before the first dam was poured or heavy equipment carved roads into the forests of northern Quebec, government officials, engineers, and politicians were already looking north. As early as the 1950s, the province of Quebec had begun quietly exploring the vast hydroelectric potential of the James Bay region, a remote and largely untouched expanse of boreal forest, lakes, and powerful rivers stretching toward Hudson Bay.

For Quebec, energy was more than just electricity. It was seen as a cornerstone of economic development and provincial identity. In the post-war years, the province was eager to modernize, industrialize, and, crucially, control its own resources. Northern Quebec, with its sprawling network of rivers, held the promise of exactly that. The La Grande, Eastmain, and Rupert rivers, among others, were singled out by engineers and planners for their enormous hydro potential. Their fast-flowing waters, steady year-round volume, and relative isolation made them prime candidates for large-scale dam construction and power generation.

At the time, much of this interest unfolded behind government doors, in technical reports, and among a handful of powerful decision-makers. The people who lived along those rivers, the Cree and Inuit, whose families had hunted, fished, and trapped on the land for generations, were largely left out of the conversation. Their presence and rights were rarely, if ever, acknowledged in the early plans.

By the end of the 1960s, as Hydro-Québec grew into one of the world’s largest public utilities and the idea of energy independence took hold in the province, those quiet studies and backroom conversations began to crystallize into formal proposals. What followed in the early 1970s would forever change the landscape of the North, spark one of the most significant Indigenous rights movements in Canadian history, and reshape the relationship between development and consent in the country.

We Were Never Asked, But We Answered

In 1971, Premier Robert Bourassa stood before Quebecers with what he described as the key to the province’s future. His government announced the James Bay Hydroelectric Project, a massive $6 billion development plan that would tap into the rivers of the North to generate electricity on an unprecedented scale. For Bourassa, it was more than a construction project. It was the foundation for economic independence, job creation, and energy security in Quebec. He famously declared it would be the “project of the century.”

At the heart of the plan was the La Grande River basin, a vast network of waterways flowing west toward James Bay. The project called for the construction of a series of dams, reservoirs, and generating stations that would drastically reshape the region. Entire river systems would be diverted, vast areas of land would be flooded, and new roads and transmission lines would cut through some of the most remote territory in Canada.

The project was to be managed by Hydro-Québec, the province’s public utility, working through a new government agency known as the James Bay Development Corporation, or in French, the Société de développement de la Baie James. Construction was set to begin quickly, with little consideration for the people who lived on the land.

For the Cree and Inuit of northern Quebec, this announcement was not an exciting promise of progress. It was a direct threat to their way of life. The project would flood hunting grounds, disrupt fish and wildlife populations, and bring roads and machinery into regions that had remained largely untouched. Most importantly, the decision had been made without their knowledge, consultation, or consent.

By 1972, Cree leaders established the Grand Council of the Crees, and the Inuit formed the Northern Quebec Inuit Association. These new political organizations gave their communities a united voice, determined to fight back against the project that threatened their land, their hunting grounds, and their way of life.

“We were never asked. The government had already made up its mind,” recalled Billy Diamond, the young Cree leader from Waskaganish who became Grand Chief at just 21. “They saw the land as empty. But for us, this is home.”

The Cree and Inuit quickly realized that legal action was their most immediate tool. Their lawyers filed for an injunction in Quebec Superior Court, arguing that the project violated their rights. It was a bold move for small, remote communities facing the full weight of the Quebec government and Hydro-Québec.

“We were told that development was inevitable,” said Philip Awashish, a Cree negotiator from Mistissini. “But we also knew that we had rights, even if the government didn’t recognize them yet. We had to test that in court.”

In the fall of 1973, their case landed before Justice Albert Malouf. For weeks, Cree and Inuit witnesses, elders, and legal experts presented evidence of their relationship to the land, their dependence on the rivers and wildlife, and the devastating impact the project would bring.

On November 15, 1973, Justice Malouf delivered his decision. It was a shock to many. He ruled that construction on the James Bay Project must stop, citing the serious and irreparable harm it posed to the Cree and Inuit and recognizing that their rights had not been extinguished.

“We were relieved, but also cautious,” remembered Charlie Watt, one of the Inuit negotiators. “The decision was a victory, but we knew the fight wasn’t over.”

In fact, the Malouf injunction was quickly overturned on appeal, and construction resumed. But the legal and political pressure created by the resistance could not be ignored. The decision had forced governments and Hydro-Québec to the negotiating table, setting the stage for the first modern Indigenous land claims agreement in Canada.

For the Cree and Inuit, the message was clear. As Billy Diamond would later say, “We may be small in number, but when we stand together, we are not powerless.”

A New Kind of Agreement: Negotiating the James Bay and Northern Quebec Settlement

The legal battle may not have stopped the bulldozers for long, but it sent a clear message: development in the North could not continue as if the Cree and Inuit were invisible. The court case had cracked open the door, and Cree and Inuit leaders intended to keep pushing.

Throughout 1973 and 1974, political pressure mounted. International media began paying attention, exposing how Quebec’s grand plans for energy expansion came at the expense of Indigenous rights and the environment. The Supreme Court of Canada’s decision in the Calder case, which confirmed that Aboriginal title existed in Canadian law, only strengthened the Cree and Inuit position. They now had legal precedent on their side.

Quebec and Canada, recognizing the risk of prolonged legal uncertainty and public controversy, agreed to sit down with Indigenous leaders to negotiate a formal settlement. These were not easy talks. The relationship between the Cree and Inuit and the provincial government had been badly strained by years of exclusion and disregard. But both sides understood that a different approach was needed.

The negotiations that followed were groundbreaking in Canadian history. Rather than settling for compensation alone, Cree and Inuit leaders demanded a comprehensive agreement that would recognize their rights, protect their way of life, and provide a real voice in the development of their homeland.

In November 1975, after months of tense discussions, the parties signed the James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement (JBNQA). It was the first modern land claims settlement in Canada, often described as the country’s first “modern treaty.” The agreement recognized Cree and Inuit rights to land, hunting, and resource use. It also provided financial compensation, along with guarantees for education, health services, and local governance.

Importantly, the agreement created a new framework for how development would proceed in the region. It required consultation, environmental and social protections, and the establishment of joint management boards where Cree and Inuit representatives would have a seat at the table.

While not perfect, the JBNQA marked a turning point. It showed that Indigenous peoples could no longer be sidelined in the decisions that shaped their lands and futures. It was a turning point that would echo far beyond the rivers of northern Quebec, influencing policies, courtrooms, and resource projects across the country.

For the Cree and Inuit, the agreement was both a compromise and a hard-won recognition. The hydro project would proceed, but so would their political and legal presence. The days of ignoring northern communities were over, at least in principle. The struggle had forced a new conversation about development, consent, and the meaning of rights in Canada.

A New Era for Resource Development and Environmental Law

The James Bay Project changed the rules for resource development across Canada. For the Cree and Inuit, it marked a turning point in their fight for rights and recognition. For governments, industries, and the courts, it exposed a new reality: Indigenous peoples could no longer be excluded from the decisions shaping their homelands.

The James Bay and Northern Quebec Agreement was the first formal recognition that Indigenous peoples could not be sidelined from decisions that reshaped their territories. Though the project moved ahead, it did so with the Cree and Inuit at the table, negotiating terms, protections, and long-term benefits. It was an imperfect victory, but it set a precedent that has echoed through every major project since.

“Before James Bay, development just happened to us,” Cree leader Philip Awashish later reflected. “After James Bay, we had a voice, and we had rights that governments could no longer ignore.”

The Roots of Environmental Assessment in Canada

The project also exposed a glaring gap in Canadian law: the absence of a structured process to assess and regulate the environmental and social impacts of development. At the time, there was no formal environmental assessment (EA) regime in place. The Malouf decision and the public outcry over James Bay helped force that conversation to the forefront.

By the late 1970s and early 1980s, Canada began building its first generation of environmental assessment policies and legislation, aimed at preventing the kind of exclusion and environmental risks seen in James Bay. Provinces developed their own EA regimes, and at the federal level, processes evolved that would eventually lead to the Canadian Environmental Assessment Act of 1992, and later, its modern successor, the Impact Assessment Act.

Critically, these processes began to embed the principle that Indigenous peoples must be consulted on projects affecting their territories. Over time, this evolved into the legal duty to consult and accommodate, now a cornerstone of resource development in Canada.

A Legacy of Inclusion and Unfinished Work

The James Bay conflict also shifted public expectations. Environmental groups, legal scholars, and Indigenous organizations increasingly demanded that development could not come at the expense of rights, cultures, or ecosystems. The project sparked decades of legal and political change that continue to shape Canada’s approach to energy, mining, and infrastructure development today.

For the Cree and Inuit, the James Bay experience was both a loss and a victory. The rivers were diverted, but their rights were recognized. The land was altered, but their place at the decision-making table was secured.

The so-called project of the century may have begun without them, but it ensured that no future development in Canada would happen without their voice.
