On December 7, 1941, Japanese fighter planes attacked Pearl Harbor, striking the U.S. Pacific Fleet and killing over 2,400 people. For years, the United States had stood apart from the war tearing through Europe, refusing to support its allies as fascism spread across continents. But the moment bombs fell on American soil, neutrality vanished.

The war was no longer foreign. It was personal and urgent.

Within months, the U.S. mobilized not just its own territory, but Canada’s North. Engineers and soldiers crossed into Sahtú Dene land to launch the Canol Project: a massive, hastily planned wartime pipeline across Indigenous territory.

The Canol Project, short for Canadian Oil, was a massive U.S.-led wartime infrastructure operation launched in 1942. It involved the construction of a 600-kilometre pipeline from Norman Wells, Northwest Territories to a newly built refinery in Whitehorse, Yukon, along with a road, pump stations, telegraph lines, and airfields. Designed to supply fuel for Allied military operations in Alaska during World War II, the project was completed at breakneck speed under harsh subarctic conditions. It cost over $130 million USD, operated for just 13 months, and was abandoned in 1945, leaving behind widespread environmental damage and a legacy of disruption on Sahtú Dene and Métis lands.

It was sold as a defensive necessity, designed to protect Alaska and supply oil for Allied forces. But for the Sahtú Dene and Métis, it was something else entirely: a foreign occupation launched without warning, consent, or respect.

Allies, Except at Home: How the U.S. Entered Sahtú Lands Without Consent

By 1941, Canada was already deep in the fight against Nazi Germany. As a Dominion of the British Empire, it had declared war on Germany in 1939, two years before the United States, and had already suffered losses in Europe and Hong Kong. Canadian soldiers were dying alongside British and French forces while America watched from the sidelines, hesitant to get involved in a “foreign war.”

That changed abruptly on December 7, 1941, when Japan attacked Pearl Harbor. With its Pacific fleet crippled and Alaska suddenly exposed, the United States panicked. The war was now not just a distant obligation, it was on their doorstep. And they needed oil.

Canada’s North, particularly the Norman Wells oilfield, became strategically critical. To feed the war machine, the U.S. scrambled to build a secure, continental supply chain: pipelines, refineries, roads, and airstrips, projects like the Alaska Highway and the Canol Project. But there was a problem: these plans extended deep into Canadian territory, including the Sahtú Dene and Métis territories that Canada occupied.

The Permission That Was and Wasn’t Granted

In early 1942, diplomatic negotiations between Ottawa and Washington moved swiftly. Canada, under Prime Minister William Lyon Mackenzie King, agreed to allow U.S. military forces and contractors to operate on Canadian soil to support the war effort. These permissions were granted quietly, under the logic of shared Allied defense, and often in informal or secretive agreements rather than transparent treaties.

The Canol Project was approved under this broader umbrella with the understanding that the U.S. would build the pipeline, refinery, and road, then hand them over to Canada once the war ended. But while Ottawa and Washington exchanged notes and signed off on logistics, no one consulted the people whose land would be most directly affected.

The Sahtú Dene and Métis were never asked. No treaty was negotiated. No consent was sought. No compensation was offered.

“We weren’t told about any agreement. The government in Ottawa didn’t ask us, and the Americans didn’t care. They just came.”

— Elder from Tulita, fictionalized from oral history accounts

To the federal government, the Northwest Territories were a remote, sparsely populated frontier; administered, but not protected. The colonial view saw Indigenous land as unoccupied and unentitled. So when American bulldozers tore through traplines, rivers, and ancestral trails, it wasn’t seen as a violation, it was seen as progress.

“Canada said yes on our behalf. But we never said yes. That’s not partnership. That’s dispossession.”

— Sahtú youth leader, paraphrased from community consultation sessions

The diplomatic permissions granted to the U.S. may have had strategic logic in the halls of Ottawa and Washington. But on the ground in the Sahtú, they looked and felt like something else entirely: a land invasion, dressed in Allied flags.

“We Were Just in the Way”: The Sahtú Dene and the Canol Invasion

Before the bulldozers came, the Sahtú Dene and Métis lived along the Mackenzie River as they had for generations, hunting moose and caribou, trapping in winter, gathering medicines and berries, and moving with the rhythms of the land. Their knowledge of the land was intimate, built over thousands of years. The river was their highway, the forest their storehouse, the mountains their sanctuary.

Then came the war.

In the winter of 1942–43, without warning or consultation, thousands of U.S. Army personnel and civilian contractors arrived in the Sahtú region. They came with tanks, trucks, sawmills, cranes, and a timetable dictated not by the land, but by the Pentagon. In just months, the quiet muskeg trails and game-rich valleys were gouged open for a road and pipeline that the locals had neither asked for nor agreed to.

The Sahtú Dene watched their traplines bulldozed, their cabins burned or appropriated, and their trails swallowed by the mud churned up by heavy equipment. Some tried to speak to the American soldiers, but were met with indifference or misunderstanding. Others were hired as guides, laborers, or hunters, paid in canned food, tobacco, or wages that rarely matched the value of the knowledge they provided.

Many elders remember the Canol Project as a time of chaos and disruption. In oral histories collected decades later, Sahtú Dene spoke of game animals fleeing the noise, of fish poisoned by oil spills, and of being treated as invisible on their own land. One elder, recalling the project, said:

“They never asked. They just came and built everything. We were just in the way.”

The trauma was not only environmental. The arrival of outside workers brought disease, alcohol, and new forms of dependency. Families were displaced, traditions interrupted, and social bonds tested by the sudden pressure of war-driven industrialization.

For the Sahtú Dene, the Canol Project was not just a pipeline, it was an invasion. A foreshadowing of what development would mean for Indigenous peoples across the North: uninvited, disruptive, and dirty.

A Toxic Legacy: What the Canol Project Left Behind

The rusting skeleton of World War II ambition still scars the landscape to this day. What remains today is one of North America’s longest and most remote trails. But far from being just a wartime relic, the Canol Trail is a living reminder of colonial disregard—for Indigenous peoples, for ecosystems, and for the land itself.

The project cut through Sahtú Dene and Métis land without consultation or consent. It disrupted wildlife migration routes, desecrated sacred landscapes, and left behind a toxic legacy that is still being cleaned up decades later. When the Canol pipeline shut down in 1945, barely a year after it began operating, it left a toxic footprint that has taken generations to fully understand, let alone address.

Even during its short operation, the Canol pipeline leaked repeatedly, due to rushed construction, poor welding, and the brutal conditions of the North. Crude oil and fuel spills contaminated snowpack, rivers, and ground. The pipeline broke down over 80 times in just 13 months, according to post-war assessments. Emergency repairs often involved simply rerouting the flow around broken sections, leaving spills to soak into the muskeg and taiga.

But the real damage came after the war.

Thousands of drums of oil and fuel were abandoned along the route. Some leaked over time, their contents seeping into the soil. Vehicles, generators, and fuel tanks were left to rust and rupture. Old batteries oozed lead and acid. And hundreds of wooden buildings, many insulated with asbestos, were left to decay in place, slowly releasing hazardous fibres into the air and ground.

The most dangerous sites included:

  • Norman Wells, where industrial operations contaminated soil and groundwater;

  • Camp Canol, near the Mackenzie River, where the remains of a U.S. military base included oil-soaked soils, PCBs, lead-based paints, and asbestos debris; and

  • The pipeline route itself, littered with corroding pipe, ruptured drums, and collapsing bridges.

The physical remains of the Canol Project are scattered across Sahtú territory, but its deepest legacy is felt in the lives and memories of the people who live there. For many, the rusting debris and poisoned soil are reminders of a time when their land and lives were treated as expendable.

“They came and went like a storm. We were left with nothing but garbage and sickness.”

— Elder from Tulita, quoted in a Canol Trail oral history project

“I remember playing near those old buildings when I was a kid. We didn’t know it was asbestos. No one warned us. Our parents didn’t even know what it was.”

— Community member, Norman Wells

“It’s our land. We should have been the first to be asked, not the last to be told.”

— Young Dene environmental worker, Fort Good Hope

The environmental damage is also tied to cultural loss. Many Sahtú Dene describe how contamination disrupted not just the ecology, but the continuity of their way of life.

“There are places my grandfather used to trap that we can’t go anymore. There’s oil in the water, and animals avoid it. It’s not just the land that was damaged, it’s our stories, our teachings.”

— Sahtú Dene trapper, Tulita

For decades, the contamination went unacknowledged and unremediated, despite ongoing concerns from Sahtú communities. Children played in abandoned buildings filled with friable asbestos. Hunters encountered oil drums in creek beds. Elders warned about fish and wildlife near certain sites.

These voices remind us that the Canol Trail is not just a remote hiking route or forgotten wartime project. For the Sahtú Dene, it is an open wound, but also a site of resilience, where a new generation is reclaiming control over how their land is restored, remembered, and respected.

Cleanup and Control: Reclaiming the Canol

For decades after the Canol Project was abandoned, the Canadian and U.S. governments largely ignored the mess left behind. The Sahtú Dene, who lived with the consequences, were left to watch the land they depended on quietly degrade, its waters tainted, its soil blackened, its hunting and trapping trails blocked by wreckage.

That began to change in the 1990s. Growing public concern about environmental contamination in the North, alongside persistent pressure from Dene leaders, finally forced the federal government to act. Sahtú Dene advocacy was the turning point, reframing the Canol not just as a wartime artifact but as a long-standing environmental injustice.

In the early 2000s, INAC (now Indigenous Services Canada) launched a series of remediation efforts focused on the most contaminated sites, starting with Camp Canol, one of the largest and most toxic hotspots. Cleanup involved:

  • Asbestos removal from collapsing buildings;

  • Excavation of hydrocarbon-contaminated soil;

  • Removal and disposal of hazardous materials like lead-acid batteries, PCBs, and rusted fuel drums; and

  • Stabilization of eroded areas and waste piles.

The cleanup has brought some hope, but also reopened wounds. Community members recall the frustration of being sidelined during the project’s construction, and again during early discussions of remediation.

Even among those now working on cleanup crews or monitoring teams, the sense of injustice lingers.

“They left us the mess, but now we’re cleaning it up. At least now our kids can be part of fixing it. That matters.”

— Dene youth technician, Canol Trail remediation project

Some of this work is now complete, but remediation is an ongoing process. The terrain is remote and expensive to access. There are still dozens of small contaminated sites scattered along the trail and pipeline corridor. Many are on or near culturally important lands, areas where Dene families have camped, hunted, or gathered medicines for generations.

Sahtú Dene Leadership in Land Restoration

Today, the Sahtú Dene are not just observers, they are leaders in the cleanup and future stewardship of the trail.

Under the Sahtú Dene and Métis Comprehensive Land Claim Agreement, signed in 1993, the people of the region have formal co-management authority over their lands. As a result, they are actively involved in decision-making about cleanup priorities, environmental monitoring, and future land use.

Local Indigenous-owned companies have been contracted for remediation work, environmental assessments, and helicopter transport. Dene youth are being trained in land stewardship, sampling, and restoration techniques, blending traditional knowledge with scientific methods. The vision is not just to clean the land, but to reconnect people to it.

There is also a growing conversation about transforming the Canol Trail into a territorial park, led by the Sahtú Renewable Resources Board and community governments. But for many Dene, this can’t happen unless the site is properly cleaned, sacred areas protected, and their role as stewards made central, not symbolic.

The Canol cleanup is far from over. But it marks a shift: from military occupation to restoring stolen Indigenous stewardship. From neglect to responsibility. From a story of erasure to one of resurgence.

Looking Forward: From Ruin to Responsibility

The story of the Canol Trail is not just about a failed pipeline. It is about who bears the burden when governments and industries make decisions without consent. It is about the slow violence of contamination, and the long memory of the land. It is about the people who are still living with that legacy, and working to heal from it.

As we confront today’s infrastructure debates - pipelines, roads, mines, and energy corridors - we have to ask: what has really changed? Are we still building in the name of national interest while ignoring the cost to Indigenous nations? Or are we finally learning to listen, to share power, and to plan for futures that do not leave people and ecosystems behind?

The Sahtú Dene never asked for a pipeline. But now, through cleanup and co-management, they are shaping what happens next. Their work to restore the Canol Trail is not just cleanup, it’s reclamation. Of land. Of sovereignty. Of voice.

In an age of infrastructure urgency and reconciliation rhetoric, the Canol Trail stands as both a warning and an invitation. Can we build urgently needed infrastructure projects while ensuring both consent from, and long term benefit for the affected nations?
