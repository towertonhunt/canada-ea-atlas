The River Was Our Teacher

Before the sickness came, the river taught us everything.

It curved through the trees like a silver thread, quiet some days, wild and roaring on others. We followed it in our canoes, the old birch ones my grandfather built with his brothers. The paddle would slice into the water, and the ripples would run ahead of us like messengers. That river carried more than fish, more than food. It carried stories, lessons, the knowledge of how to live here.

We fished those waters every season. In spring, when the ice broke up and the sun hit the surface, we’d haul in walleye so thick you could fill the boat before breakfast. The pike were long and lean, quick as shadows. The sturgeon, heavy as logs, reminded us how old the river was, how deep its memory ran.

The fish fed us, but the river gave us more than food. The water told us when the seasons were shifting. The shoreline showed us where to trap, where to set snares. The quiet places taught us how to listen. Our elders always said the land takes care of us, if we take care of it.

My grandparents learned that from their grandparents, and we learned it from them. How to live on this land, how to respect the animals, the fish, the water. Everything in balance.

We thought that balance would always hold.

But when the fish stopped swimming straight, and the babies were born with shaking hands, we knew something had poisoned the river.

The Beginning of Contamination

The pulp and paper mill in Dryden, Ontario, has been part of the town’s economy for over a century. Built in 1913, the mill was one of many industrial projects that took advantage of Northwestern Ontario’s vast forests and river systems. Over the decades, the operation grew, providing jobs and driving development in the region. Ownership changed several times, but by the early 1960s, the mill was under the control of Reed Paper, one of the major players in Canada’s forestry industry.

At that time, the mill produced bleached paper products, which required a chemical process that used mercury. Mercury was a key component in what’s called the chlor-alkali process, used to make caustic soda and chlorine for bleaching paper.

The environmental risks of using mercury were already known in scientific circles, but industry regulations were weak, and government oversight was minimal. Rivers like the English-Wabigoon system, which winds through the region and eventually passes through Grassy Narrows (Asubpeeschoseewagong) and Whitedog (Wabaseemoong), were seen as natural waste disposal systems for industry. The health of downstream ecosystems and Indigenous communities was rarely considered.

In 1962, Reed Paper began discharging wastewater containing mercury directly into the river. Over the next eight years, nearly 9,000 kilograms of mercury were released into the water. The contamination didn’t stay put. It moved downstream, building up in the fish and wildlife, and eventually into the people of Asubpeeschoseewagong and Wabaseemoong, where families had relied on the river for food, income, and cultural survival for generations.

Despite growing concerns, government officials at the time prioritized the economy over the environment. As one Ontario official reportedly stated, “We can’t let one small group of people stand in the way of progress.”

For industry, the river was a tool to fuel economic growth. For the communities who lived along its banks, it was life itself. The consequences of that disconnect are still being felt today.

The Impacts Unfold

Mercury poisoning doesn’t always show itself right away. It builds up in the body, especially through eating contaminated fish. By the time people in Asubpeeschoseewagong and Wabaseemoong realized something was wrong, the damage was already deep.

Common symptoms included:

  • Tremors and shaking in the hands and limbs;

  • Muscle weakness and loss of coordination;

  • Blurred vision and hearing loss; and

  • Numbness and tingling in fingers and toes.

The effects were especially hard on children. Many were born with developmental delays, learning difficulties, and physical disabilities. Parents began to notice their babies trembling, struggling to walk, or falling behind in speech. Entire families were living with the hidden weight of mercury poisoning.

The situation mirrored what had happened in Minamata, Japan, in the 1950s, where thousands were poisoned after a chemical company dumped mercury into the sea. That disaster, known as Minamata disease, became an international symbol of environmental negligence. But this wasn’t across the ocean. This was happening in Ontario, and to many in the community, it felt like no one was listening.

Cultural and Economic Devastation

For generations, the river had been the heart of both the economy and culture in Asubpeeschoseewagong and Wabaseemoong. Fishing wasn’t just about food, it was about identity, ceremony, and survival.

The contamination wiped that out almost overnight.

The once-thriving commercial fishing industry collapsed. Local fish couldn’t be sold in markets because of mercury levels, cutting off a major source of income. Fishing lodges that had attracted tourists from across Canada and beyond closed their doors. Jobs tied to the river vanished.

For families who fished for their own meals, the loss ran even deeper. Traditional foods were replaced with expensive store-bought goods, often of lower nutritional value. Knowledge about fishing spots, seasonal patterns, and river stewardship stopped being passed down, because the river was no longer safe.

The cultural disconnect was devastating. The river had been a teacher, a provider, and a symbol of the people’s relationship with the land. Now, it was a source of sickness and fear.

The Social Consequences

The collapse of the fishing economy and the health crisis triggered wider social challenges.

Poverty deepened as jobs disappeared. With fewer opportunities on the land, families struggled to afford basic necessities. Food insecurity became common, especially for those without easy access to affordable groceries in the remote region.

Mental health struggles rose, compounded by the grief of seeing the land suffer. Watching the river, once a source of life, turned into a carrier of poison left many feeling abandoned by the government and cut off from the traditions that had grounded them for generations.

These impacts weren’t isolated incidents. They unfolded over decades, and in many ways, they continue to this day. The mercury may have stopped flowing from the mill, but its mark on the people and the land remains.

Resistance and the Long Fight for Justice

The people of Asubpeeschoseewagong and Wabaseemoong never accepted the contamination of their river and their lives in silence. From the earliest days of the crisis, they began speaking out, demanding action, and refusing to be forgotten.

In the 1970s, as the health impacts of mercury poisoning became impossible to ignore, community members started organizing. They held protests, raised awareness with scientists and journalists, and confronted both government officials and the companies responsible. The message was clear: the mercury had poisoned not only the water and the fish, but their future.

Despite their efforts, progress was painfully slow. Governments were reluctant to take responsibility, and cleanup efforts stalled. The fishing economy never recovered, and the mercury remained in the river.

But the people didn’t give up.

The Grassy Narrows Blockade: A Stand That Endures

In 2002, members of Asubpeeschoseewagong took their resistance to the next level. They established a blockade on a logging road near their territory, physically stopping industrial activity that threatened their land.

At first, many doubted the blockade would last. But over two decades later, it’s still standing. The Grassy Narrows blockade is now one of the longest-running Indigenous land protection actions in Canada. It has become a powerful symbol of resistance, resilience, and the community’s refusal to let their land be exploited any further.

Youth Leading the Way

Over the years, a new generation has taken up the fight. Young people from Asubpeeschoseewagong have travelled across the country and around the world to share their story.

They’ve spoken at national rallies, political meetings, and even at the United Nations, putting pressure on Canada to take responsibility and act. Their message is grounded in lived experience: the mercury still lingers, the impacts are still real, and the government’s promises remain largely unfulfilled.

Delayed Promises and Incomplete Action

For decades, the Ontario government made commitments but failed to follow through. Community members pushed for a full cleanup of the English-Wabigoon river system, better health care, and long-term solutions for those suffering from mercury poisoning.

In 2017, after years of public pressure, the province finally announced an $85 million commitment to clean up the river. It was a major victory on paper, but the reality has been slower and more complicated. Progress on the cleanup has been limited, and many families still cannot safely eat the fish or rely on the river the way they once did.

Faces of the Fight

At the heart of the resistance are people like Judy Da Silva, a mother of five, grandmother, and environmental health coordinator from Asubpeeschoseewagong. For decades, Da Silva has been a tireless advocate, bringing attention to mercury poisoning, fighting for clean water, and empowering youth to stand up for their rights.

Reflecting on her childhood, Da Silva recalls the abundance of the river:

“I remember when [my father] would get a big catch, he would bring it in and then put it on ice.”

However, the prosperity was short-lived as the mercury contamination devastated the community’s way of life.

“We’ve already been poisoned. I’ve been poisoned. Simon has been poisoned. And our people sitting in the audience have been poisoned. But it’s for those little kids that are still unborn and that are just growing up to have a chance to have a good life.”

Da Silva’s commitment is fueled by her desire to protect future generations, ensuring they inherit a clean and safe environment.

It’s not only seasoned activists who carry the struggle. Young people from Asubpeeschoseewagong have led marches, spoken to the media, and brought their community’s story to the international stage. Their voices carry both the pain of what was lost and the determination to reclaim what can still be protected.

Paris Meekis, a youth activist, emphasizes the urgency of their mission:

“We will not stand by and let people die.”

Jenae Turtle, another youth leader, expresses frustration over unfulfilled promises:

“Where’s that mercury [care] home that the government promised us a long time ago? We’ve been fighting for a long time and nothing has been done.”

Steve Fobister Jr., also known as Darwin, shares his personal challenges:

“I have trouble speaking. I have speech impairments. I have learning disabilities. But like my late grandfather, Steve Fobister Sr., I will never give up until our waters are clean and our fish are safe to eat.”

These young leaders embody the resilience of their community, refusing to let their story fade and continuing the fight for justice and environmental restoration.

Where Things Stand Today

It has been more than 60 years since mercury first entered the English-Wabigoon river system. The pulp mill in Dryden has changed ownership several times, and the mercury dumping stopped in 1970, but for Asubpeeschoseewagong and Wabaseemoong, the impacts have never really ended.

Since the Ontario government announced an $85 million cleanup commitment in 2017, there has been slow movement to address the contamination. Scientific studies have been done, and some cleanup work has begun, but the process has been frustratingly slow for the communities most affected.

Parts of the river system remain unsafe. Many families still cannot eat the fish, and mercury continues to show up in the bodies of residents, including children.

Independent researchers and community members have repeatedly called for a more aggressive cleanup plan that prioritizes the safety and well-being of the people who depend on the river.

The Fight for a Mercury Treatment Center

Beyond cleaning up the river, Asubpeeschoseewagong has been calling for a dedicated mercury treatment center in the community. Many residents live with long-term health effects from mercury poisoning, yet accessing proper care often means travelling far from home.

A local treatment center would allow people to receive medical support without leaving their families, culture, and territory behind. It would also be a tangible sign that governments are finally listening to the needs of the community.

So far, those calls have been met with promises, but not the action Asubpeeschoseewagong is demanding.

A Community That Refuses to Be Forgotten

Despite the ongoing challenges, Asubpeeschoseewagong continues to assert their rights and fight for accountability. The blockade still stands. Youth leaders still raise their voices. Community members continue to remind Canada that the river may look quiet, but the damage and the resistance run deep.

The people of Asubpeeschoseewagong have made it clear: they are not asking for sympathy. They are demanding justice, respect, and the right to live safely on their own land.

The River Remembers

The English-Wabigoon river still winds through the forests of Northwestern Ontario, the same way it always has. On the surface, it looks quiet, peaceful, unchanged. But beneath the current, the river holds memory. It remembers the mercury. It remembers the promises broken. It remembers the people who have refused to be silenced.

For Asubpeeschoseewagong and Wabaseemoong, that memory lives in every family that still can’t fish safely, in every child carrying the hidden weight of mercury poisoning, and in every voice that continues to speak out for justice.

What happened here is not an isolated story. It’s what happens when industry is allowed to operate without proper oversight. When governments value short-term economic gains over the health of communities and the land itself. The pulp mill in Dryden dumped mercury into the river because it was cheaper, faster, and easier than managing waste responsibly. The government looked the other way because they believed the jobs and profits were worth the risk.

Decades later, those same attitudes still show up in legislation. In Ontario, Bill 5, passed in 2025, stripped away key protections from the province’s environmental assessment process. It made it easier for industry to push projects through without fully understanding or addressing the risks to water, land, and people.

At the federal level, Bill C-5 seeks to weaken public participation requirements and scale back the role of Indigenous communities in environmental decision-making. These laws send a clear message: when governments weaken oversight, the same patterns repeat. The land gets exploited. Communities get sidelined. And the consequences, like the mercury in the river, linger for generations.

Asubpeeschoseewagong has already lived through the worst-case scenario of what happens when development moves ahead without consent, caution, or accountability. The community’s story is both a warning and a lesson. It’s a reminder that rivers carry more than water. They carry history. They carry pain. But they also carry strength.

The people of Asubpeeschoseewagong have shown that strength for over six decades. They have fought for their river, their health, and their rights. Their story will not be forgotten.

The river remembers what was done to it.

And so do they.
