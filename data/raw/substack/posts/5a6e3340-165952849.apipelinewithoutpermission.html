In the early 1970s, Canada’s federal government was preparing to greenlight one of the most ambitious infrastructure projects in its history: a 1,200-kilometre natural gas pipeline that would snake through the Northwest Territories, following the Mackenzie River valley from the Beaufort Sea to Alberta. Backed by a powerful consortium of oil and gas companies, the Mackenzie Valley Pipeline was seen as a gateway to Arctic resource wealth and national energy security.

But there was a problem, the land wasn’t empty.

The Mackenzie Valley Pipeline promised energy and economic growth, but it also threatened the lands, waters, and lifeways of the Dene, Gwich’in, Métis, and Inuvialuit peoples who had lived there for millennia. Resistance from these Nations led to the Berger Inquiry, a groundbreaking public review that marked Canada’s first major environmental assessment process. But it was more than a bureaucratic milestone. For the first time, Indigenous voices were not only heard but central to decision-making. Their testimonies challenged assumptions about development, territory, and consent, setting a new precedent for how Canada engages with both the environment and the original stewards of the land.

Echoes in the Tundra

Every spring, the people watched the hills.

They waited for the first dark line of caribou to crest the ridgeline, like beads moving across the land. Grandmothers boiled snow water in old kettles and told the children to listen; not with ears, but with the body.

“You’ll feel them in your chest before you see them,” they’d say. “The land remembers their footsteps.”

The caribou were not just animals. They were ancestors in another form, bringing meat, hide, and spirit. Their migration taught patience, taught movement, taught balance. And always, just as the caribou returned, the ice on the Mackenzie would break; slow at first, then all at once, the great river breathing again after a long sleep.

But one year, something was different.

The river groaned too early. Planes came. Roads followed. Survey lines cut across trails older than memory. In places where caribou once crossed in thousands, there were now fences of orange tape, thudding machines, and strangers taking core samples of the earth like it was theirs to dissect.

Elders gathered and said, if the caribou stop coming, the land is trying to tell us something.

Young people listened. Some left school to speak for the land. One of them was named Frank. He had watched the caribou every year of his life. When he stood to testify, he didn’t talk about politics or economics. He talked about water, about migration, about what happens when the river is strangled and the caribou turn away.

Because when the caribou stop, it is not just their silence. It is a silence in the people, too.

From Dene Resistance to a National Reckoning

The Dene, Gwich’in, Métis, and Inuvialuit peoples had lived in the Mackenzie Valley for thousands of years. Their lives followed the migrations of caribou and the seasonal rhythms of rivers and ice. They had treaties, but no settled land claims. And crucially, they had not been asked.

By 1974, the Dene Nation (then called the Indian Brotherhood of the Northwest Territories) had become increasingly organized, politically active, and clear in their demands: no development without land rights and consent. They viewed the pipeline not just as a threat to the environment, but as part of a broader project of colonization; an attempt to permanently industrialize the North without Indigenous permission.

At the time, Pierre Trudeau’s federal government was under pressure to balance economic expansion with rising social awareness, particularly around Indigenous rights. The 1969 White Paper, which had attempted to eliminate Indian status and “assimilate” Indigenous peoples, had already triggered a backlash and a rise in Indigenous political mobilization across the country.

Amid growing resistance in the North, and an awareness that a major misstep could spark deeper conflict, the government made a surprising decision: to pause and listen.

Enter Justice Thomas Berger

In 1974, Justice Thomas Berger, a B.C. Supreme Court judge with a background in civil rights law and Indigenous justice, was appointed to lead a formal inquiry into the pipeline. The Mackenzie Valley Pipeline Inquiry, often simply called the Berger Inquiry, would become the first of its kind in Canada: a sweeping, multi-year public review of an energy megaproject that put environmental and Indigenous concerns at the centre.

Berger didn’t just host hearings in Ottawa or Yellowknife. He traveled to 35 communities across the North, many only accessible by boat or plane, and held hearings in English, Dene, Gwich’in, Inuvialuktun and Métis languages. People spoke in gyms, church basements, and community halls. Over 1,000 individuals gave testimony, trappers, elders, youth, hunters, community leaders, many for the first time in a public forum.

The Berger Inquiry was unlike anything Canada had seen. In town after town, Indigenous people stood before a government-appointed judge and flipped the power dynamic, using public testimony not only to voice opposition but to reassert sovereignty. They did not speak the language of policy and permits. They spoke of relationships; to land, to water, to ancestors, and of what would be lost if a pipeline cut through the heart of it all.

The Testimonies That Changed Everything

Among the most searing testimonies came from Chief Frank T’Seleie, then a 24-year-old leader from Fort Good Hope. In Fort Franklin (now Délı̨nę), standing before Justice Berger and representatives of powerful oil companies, he delivered a speech that would come to symbolize the Indigenous resistance of the era.

He looked directly at the representatives of the oil companies and said:

“You are committing genocide against us.”

It was a shocking statement, not made lightly. T’Seleie went on to explain that while settlers had once used rifles and disease to displace Indigenous people, the pipeline was a new form of destruction: one cloaked in business suits, backed by money and machines. It would devastate the land and, with it, the people who depended on it for physical, cultural, and spiritual sustenance.

He said:

“You are talking about taking away from us the very things that keep us alive... We are not opposed to development. We are opposed to being destroyed by it.”

T’Seleie’s message was not just resistance, it was vision. He didn’t call for isolation or denial of progress. He called for recognition, land rights, and a seat at the table. For the North to be developed, yes, but with Indigenous consent, control, and protection of traditional lifeways.

A Gwich’in elder stated:

“You talk about gas and oil. We talk about caribou and fish. When you hurt the water, you hurt us. What you call wilderness is our home.”

A Métis hunter from Fort Simpson testified:

“We don’t want to stop change. But we want to be alive to see it. Don’t ask us to trade our future for your fuel.”

An Inuvialuit woman shared:

“I speak for my grandchildren. If the land is gone, what stories will we tell them? What food will they eat?”

Their messages echoed the same core truth: the land is not a resource, it is a relative.

Berger’s Verdict

These testimonies were not only persuasive, they were transformative. Berger later acknowledged that hearing from those most directly affected changed his own view of the project. In the end, he didn’t just deliver a technical report. He delivered a moral judgment.

Justice Berger went far beyond analyzing economic feasibility or engineering challenges. His conclusion was rooted in a moral reckoning: that no project, no matter how profitable, should proceed if it threatens the survival, culture, and rights of the people who live on the land.

He recognized that the pipeline posed irreversible risks not only to fragile northern ecosystems but to Indigenous nations whose voices had long been ignored in Canada's development agenda.

“No pipeline should be built now through the Mackenzie Valley. We must first settle the land claims of the native people, and we must reach agreement with them on terms of development.”
— Justice Thomas Berger, Final Report, 1977

Berger called for a 10-year moratorium not simply to delay construction, but to allow time for Indigenous land claims to be resolved and for Northern communities to shape their own future. His judgment reframed development as a question not of whether it can be done, but whether it should be, and who gets to decide.

The Birth of Environmental Assessment in Canada

Before the Berger Inquiry, there were no legal requirements in Canada to consider environmental impacts or consult the public, let alone Indigenous peoples, before launching massive industrial projects. Development decisions were made behind closed doors, driven largely by economic interests and federal priorities.

The Mackenzie Valley Pipeline changed that.

In appointing Justice Thomas Berger to lead the inquiry in 1974, the federal government was responding not just to local resistance but to a growing awareness across the country: Canada could no longer afford to ignore the environmental and social costs of development. The inquiry broke new ground by considering not just economic benefits, but cultural, ecological, and community well-being.

Berger’s 1977 final report called for more than just a pause on the pipeline. It recommended permanent reforms: that any major development project in Canada should undergo a full environmental assessment, and that such assessments must include meaningful Indigenous and public participation.

Berger’s 1977 final report laid the groundwork for Canada’s first formal environmental assessment process: the Environmental Assessment and Review Process Guidelines Order (EARPGO, 1977). While EARPGO marked a critical turning point, its limitations eventually led to the creation of the Canadian Environmental Assessment Act (CEAA) in 1992, later revised in 2012 to expand federal responsibilities.

Most recently, Canada introduced the Impact Assessment Act (2019), which more explicitly embeds Indigenous rights, public participation, and sustainability into federal project reviews.

Today, however, we face a new crossroads. Proposed legislation such as Bill C-5 at the federal level and Bill 5 in Ontario seeks to streamline environmental assessments by reducing what some view as regulatory “red tape.” Yet many fear that these changes could undermine decades of hard-won progress, weakening environmental oversight and eroding Indigenous engagement that began with Berger’s vision for just and inclusive development.

Each of these steps traces its lineage back to Berger.

When Listening Became Law

The Berger Inquiry became a model for environmental review worldwide. It showed that development decisions could be paused, not to stop progress, but to make it more just, informed, and inclusive. And critically, it reframed Indigenous peoples not as “stakeholders,” but as rights-holders with inherent authority over their lands.

It marked the moment when Canada began, haltingly, imperfectly, to recognize that environmental protection and Indigenous consent are not obstacles to development, but the foundation of sustainable futures.
