The Lake in September

In September, Lake Huron turns the colour of hammered copper in the late afternoon. The wind comes off the water cold and smells of fish and distance. If you grew up on that shore, you know that smell the way you know your own hands.

Clifford George grew up on Stoney Point. His family had been there as long as anyone could remember, fishing the lake, trapping along the creek corridors, burying their dead in the sandy ground above the shoreline. The Anishinabbe name for the place was Aazhoodena. It meant something like “the other village,” the quieter settlement of the Kettle and Stony Point First Nation, separated from the main reserve by a few kilometres of boreal edge and farmland.

In the spring of 1942, men in suits came to the band office with maps and a piece of paper. They were at war, they said. Canada needed the land for a training camp. It would be temporary, they said. The government would pay $15 per acre. And when the war was over, the land would return.

The families of Aazhoodena were given thirty days to pack and leave.

Clifford George’s family moved to Kettle Point, the main reserve. They took what they could carry. They left the rest, the gardens, the traplines, the graves. They left the graves.

The war ended in 1945, but the. The land was not returned did not come back.

It was not returned, and did not come back in 1950, 1960, or 1970. The Canadian military continued to run summer cadet training on the base through the 1990s. The burial grounds sat beneath the parade square and the vehicle yard. The families of Aazhoodena waited, wrote letters, filed requests, hired lawyers, and were ignored for fifty years.

By 1993, some of them had stopped waiting. They moved back to the land.

Dudley George was one of them. He was 38 years old in the summer of 1995. He was known in the community as a gentle, funny man, the kind of person who made people laugh. He was not a politician or a lawyer. He was someone who believed, that the land was theirs, and that fifty years was long enough to wait.

On the night of September 6, 1995, Dudley George walked out of Ipperwash Provincial Park carrying a stick. An OPP sniper named Ken Deane fired three shots. George was hit and badly wounded. His sister Carolyn and brother Pierre loaded him into a car and tried to drive to the hospital. The police stopped them and held them for over an hour.

Dudley George was declared dead at Strathroy Hospital on September 7, 1995.

He was the first Indigenous person shot to death by police in Canada in over a century.

What the Government Took, and Why

The Stoney Point Reserve along the Lake Huron shoreline had stood for generations before 1942. It was a tiny fraction of the Chippewas of Kettle and Stoney Point’s Chippewas of Kettle and Stoney Point’s traditional territory that was taken by the British Crown through the coercive numbered treaty process of the 19th century (Treaty 29).

The further expropriation in 1942that year happened under the War Measures Act, the same legislation that would later be used to intern Japanese Canadians and, a generation later, to suspend civil liberties during the FLQ crisis. It was a blunt instrument, designed for emergencies, with no requirement for negotiation or consent. The government offered fifteen dollars an acre, a price the band rejected. The government took the land anyway.

Thirty-one families were relocated. Homes were moved or bulldozed—the military erected fences around the burial ground. The base was named Camp Ipperwash.

The promise to return the land was informal, the kind of assurance that sounds reasonable in wartime and disappears into bureaucratic fog once the shooting stops. There was no binding legal commitment, no sunset clause, no reversion mechanism in the expropriation order. And so when the war ended, and federal priorities shifted, the land simply stayed federal. Year after year, through Liberal and Conservative governments alike, requests to honour the original commitment were acknowledged and shelved.

By the 1980s, the Stoney Point people had made the issue a formal political demand. By 1993, a group of them, Clifford George among them, had moved onto the base itself. In July 1995, they occupied the barracks and evicted the military. The cadets left. The Stoney Pointers stayed.

Then, on Labour Day weekend, a group of about 35 community members, including women and teenage boys, occupied the adjacent Ipperwash Provincial Park. They had waited until the seasonal campers had gone. They came peacefully to draw attention to the burial ground within the park boundary and to the decades-old land claim that no government had seen fit to resolve.

What happened next says a great deal about who Ontario thought it was in 1995, and who it thought the Stoney Point people were.

“I Want the Fucking Indians Out of the Park”

On September 5, the day after the park occupation began, Premier Mike Harris convened a meeting of senior Ontario officials, including the Solicitor General and senior staff. The meeting was held at the Premier’s office at Queen’s Park.

The notes from that meeting record its conclusion plainly: the province would take steps to remove the occupiers as soon as possible.

Former Attorney General Charles Harnick testified at the Ipperwash Inquiry years later that Premier Harris had said, at that meeting: “I want the fuck—ing Indians out of the park.”

Harris denied making the statement. Justice Sidney B. Linden, who chaired the inquiry, found that Harris had likely said it, though he concluded the comment had limited direct effect on the OPP’s tactical decisions that night. That conclusion has been debated. What is not debated is that within hours of the Queen’s Park meeting, the OPP began mobilizing a tactical response, including the deployment of the Tactics and Rescue Unit, the force’s elite tactical team, to Ipperwash.

The OPP also received intelligence from CSIS, Canada’s domestic spy agency, that there were weapons on the base. Documents declassified and reported on in 2025 show that CSIS had been actively investigating what it internally called “Native extremism” at Ipperwash in the weeks before the shooting. The characterization is striking. These were people trying to reclaim land their families had been evicted from by force. Land that represented a tiny fraction of their Traditional Territory already taken by the Crown. , landLand with a promise attached to it, land containing their ancestors’ graves. CSIS saw terrorism. Justice Linden later found the protesters had no guns. They had bats, sticks, and stones.

On the night of September 6, the OPP staged a nighttime confrontation near the park entrance. A car and a school bus moved out of the park. Acting Sergeant Ken Deane, positioned with a four-man sharpshooter team, fired at Dudley George. His defence, at trial, was that George had been carrying a rifle. The judge rejected that claim outright, finding that Deane had invented it to conceal the fact that he had shot an unarmed man.

Deane was convicted of criminal negligence causing death. He received a conditional sentence of two years less a day, to be served in the community, plus 180 hours of community service. He did not serve a single day in prison. He was later ordered to resign from the OPP. He died in a car accident in February 2006, while awaiting his turn to testify at the Ipperwash Inquiry.

For eight years after the shooting, under the Harris government, there was no official inquiry. The George family and their lawyers pushed for one. Human rights organizations called for one. The answer, every time, was no.

When Dalton McGuinty’s Liberals came to power in 2003, they launched the inquiry almost immediately. Justice Linden’s final report was delivered in May 2007.

The Inquiry, the Findings, and What They Mean

The Ipperwash Inquiry heard four years of testimony. It examined the political interference leading up to the shooting, the OPP’s tactical choices, the behaviour of individual officers, and the broader systemic failures that produced the crisis.

Justice Linden’s findings were unsparing. He found that some OPP officers had been ignorant of Indigenous history and the legal context of the protest. He found that others held explicitly racist views, describing Indigenous people in terms the inquiry report would not repeat. He found that the rush to remove the occupiers, driven at least in part by political pressure from Queen’s Park, had compressed the timeline in ways that preempted foreclosed non-violent options.

He also found something larger: that Ipperwash was not an isolated incident. It was the product of a relationship between the Crown and Indigenous peoples in Ontario that was broken at its foundations.

The inquiry recommended that the province return Ipperwash Provincial Park to the Kettle and Stony Point First Nation. It called for improved police training on Indigenous rights and history. It called for a formal First Nations consultation process before police deployments related to Indigenous occupations. It recommended changes to how Ontario handled longstanding land disputes, so that communities would not have to occupy land to get the government’s attention.

It did not recommend criminal charges against Mike Harris. The question of whether the premier’s statement had legally caused the shooting was, Linden found, too attenuated to establish.

What the inquiry could not do was place Ipperwash within the broader legal framework taking shape in Canadian courts at the time. That work was happening elsewhere.

In 1997, the Supreme Court of Canada decided Delgamuukw v British Columbia, recognizing that Aboriginal title was a real and enforceable interest in land, not merely a historical claim to be managed by the Crown. In 2004, Haida Nation v British Columbia established that the duty to consult arose before any Crown decision that could adversely affect Aboriginal rights, even before those rights were proven in court. Together, these decisions were reshaping what governments were legally required to do before acting on Indigenous lands.

Ipperwash sits awkwardly in this legal arc. The Anishinabbe of Kettle and Stoney Point people were not arguing Aboriginal title. They were arguing something more basic: that the federal government had made a promise, taken their land under wartime emergency powers, and then broken that promise for fifty years. No consultation framework, no matter how well designed, addresses what to do when the Crown simply refuses to follow through on a commitment it made in 1942.

What Haida Nation and the cases that followed it do establish is that the kind of political short-circuit that happened at Ipperwash — (i.e. a premier deciding at a meeting that protesters need to be removed, with police deployed the same night, without any genuine engagement with the underlying land rights issue) — is exactly what the duty to consult is designed to prevent. The Crown cannot run out the clock on Indigenous rights by ignoring them until the situation becomes acute, and then treating the resulting protest as a policing problem.

That lesson, learned from Ipperwash, is one that Canadian governments have been slow to absorb.

What Came After, and What Hasn’t

In December 2007, the Ontario government announced it would return Ipperwash Provincial Park to the Chippewas of Kettle and Stony Point First Nation. The formal settlement was completed on April 14, 2016, with a payment of $95 million. The park, with its long sandy beach on Lake Huron, is now Kettle and Stony Point territory.

Camp Ipperwash, where the families have been living since 1993, is a different story. The federal government still controls the former military base. It is contaminated with unexploded ordnance, grenades, and artillery shells left over from decades of military training. As of the most recent reporting, decontamination is expected to take another 25 years. The families are living on land that the government is in the process of cleaning up. They are living, in other words, on a site that the federal government made dangerous, in order to reclaim land their families were removed from by force.

The burial ground at the former park, whose presence the Stoney Point people had been trying to protect all along, was confirmed by archaeological survey in 2010. Fifteen years after Dudley George was shot for trying to protect it.

Justice Linden’s policing recommendations were partially adopted. The OPP developed new protocols for Indigenous occupations, an approach focused on communication and patience rather than tactical escalation. Those protocols were put to a significant test at Caledonia in 2006, where a Six Nations land dispute stretched on for years with a notably different policing posture than Ipperwash. Whether that difference was due to Ipperwash’s lessons, or to the legal and political environment that had shifted around the duty to consult, is hard to separate.

What has not changed is the underlying dynamic. Canada still has hundreds of unresolved land claims. Governments still routinely allow them to sit unaddressed for decades, and then express surprise when communities take direct action to assert what they believe is theirs.

Grand Chief Joel Abram of the Association of Iroquois and Allied Indians put it plainly in 2020: “The root of the problem is refusal to acknowledge Indigenous people’s rights to the land, and the colonialism that’s happened.”

Thirty years later, that root has not been touched.

The Pattern and the Present

Ipperwash is an Ontario story, but its shape is recognizable from every other post in this series. A government takes land under pressure or emergency powers, with a promise to make it right later. Later never comes. The people whose land was taken exhaust every formal channel available to them. Then they act. And the state responds with force, or surveillance, or both, before ever seriously engaging with the underlying grievance.

What changes over time is not the pattern but the legal framework around it. The Berger Inquiry in the 1970s built the case for environmental assessment and Indigenous participation in project reviews. Haida Nation in 2004 created a legally enforceable duty to consult before Crown decisions are made. Tsilhqot’in in 2014 confirmed that Aboriginal title is a real proprietary interest that the Crown must justify infringing. Each of these developments represents a narrowing of the space governments have to ignore Indigenous rights before those rights trigger legal obligations.

Ipperwash did not produce a court decision. It produced an inquiry, and the inquiry produced recommendations, some of which were adopted. But the political dimension of what happened — a premier directing police to remove protesters from land the protesters had a legitimate claim to, based on a broken wartime promise — is not something any legal framework has yet fully addressed. The inquiry could name it. It could not remedy it.

What does remedy look like? The George family would say it looks like the camp, fully decontaminated, fully returned, the promise finally kept. The Stoney Point people who have been living there since 1993, who refused to leave even as the army did, who are living now among the unexploded shells, might say the same thing.

Dudley George died trying to make the government follow through on its word. That is not an act of extremism. It is what happens when a government breaks a promise, and then sends police to protect the broken promise from the people to whom it was made to.

This is not only history. Proposed federal legislation is currently moving to streamline environmental assessments and reduce Indigenous consultation requirements for infrastructure projects, on the grounds that the process is too slow. The families of Aazhoodena waited fifty years. Their land still is not fully back.

The land remembers who is still waiting. The people remember.

Anthony “Dudley” George, 1957–1995. Aazhoodena.
